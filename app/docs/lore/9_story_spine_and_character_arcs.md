# Story Spine and Character Arcs

Status: current working direction.

This document captures the story shape agreed during the May 2026 planning pass. It supersedes older notes that describe the Hidden Way as an unfinished portal or the campaign as a four-act structure.

## Core Premise

Greyharbour is the last free harbour in a world without a sun. It survives by its own light, its own oil, its own saints, its own boats, and its refusal to become another dependent province of the Bone Court.

The Bone Court does not merely want to conquer Greyharbour by force. It wants to own the flows that make the harbour possible: food, oil, ships, dead labour, diplomacy, legitimacy, and hope. Its pressure begins as blockade, bribery, poisoned supply, controlled scarcity, and diplomatic theatre. Only later does the violence become open.

The heroes leave Greyharbour because the city cannot win by simply enduring. They are sent to follow the old stories of the Hidden Way, hoping to find some uncaptured source of light, fire, power, or survival that can be brought back.

The Hidden Way is a lie.

There is no surface. There is no promised dawn. There is no true road up into light. The Hidden Way was built at the dawn of this age as a trap: a way for the Bone Court's world to funnel dangerous heroes, saints, rebels, and would-be liberators into a killing mechanism.

The final revelation must not soften that. The road was false. The hope was false. The surface was false.

What becomes true is the party.

## The Seven

The final party is:

- The PC.
- Tara, Fighter.
- Xavier, Rogue.
- Duncan, Cleric.
- Danica, Paladin.
- Tahrone, Necromancer Wizard.
- Kestrel, Lantern Patron Warlock.

Brassica is not one of the seven. She remains a major Act I character and possible future protagonist.

## Three-Act Shape

### Act I: Greyharbour Under Siege

Act I begins in the harbour under soft siege.

The Bone Court is pressing Greyharbour through:

- Blockaded roads and sea routes.
- Suspicious, poor-quality food shipments.
- Poisoned or adulterated lampfish oil.
- Political offers of "aid" that would create dependency.
- Pale silver, Court gifts, and private bargains.
- Sabotage of docks, channels, crews, and oil infrastructure.
- Quiet internal collusion by frightened or compromised locals.

The city is not yet simply burning. The crisis is initially diplomatic, logistical, and moral. Greyharbour is being taught that freedom is impractical.

The player is sent out toward the docks, fields, and refinery areas because the oil problem threatens the city's independence. If Greyharbour cannot make and trust its own light, it becomes dependent on Court lamps and Court mercy.

Act I ends when the Bone Court escalates. Necromancers set the outer quarters of the city on fire. That is the break: staying and protecting the harbour in the ordinary way is no longer enough.

The expedition begins as a final attempt to find something that can be brought back.

### Act II: Through the Bone Court

Act II crosses the breadth of the Bone Court.

This act should show the Court not just as a villainous army, but as a way of life: bureaucracy, etiquette, hunger, dependency, death management, inherited compromise, gifts that cannot be refused, and security bought at the cost of becoming ruled.

The party should meet people who have survived by accepting the Court's terms. The horror is not that everyone is evil. The horror is that the system makes submission reasonable.

Two later companions are introduced here:

- Tahrone, Necromancer Wizard, former High Chair of the Pale.
- Kestrel, Lantern Patron Warlock, imprisoned by the Bone Court.

These characters complicate the party's understanding of necromancy, light, faith, captivity, and power. They are not Act I harbour companions. They join after the party has entered the wider machinery of the Court.

### Act III: The Hidden Way

Act III moves beyond the Court into the wild and old imperial spaces where the Hidden Way is supposed to lead.

The party travels between luminous resting places: rest-stones. These are the only places where the party can long rest, level up, craft, and recover. For most of the campaign, they appear to be a survival network: fragments of luminous rock, old sanctuaries, or holy remnants.

In truth, the rest-stone network is part of the great trap at the end of the Hidden Way. The stones are connected to a vast creature of crystal and light. The creature has been learning the party as they travel. It knows what they can do because it has felt them grow inside its system.

At the end of Act III, the party reaches the promised end of the road and discovers that there is no surface and no salvation waiting there. They face the creature/trap itself.

The party defeats or breaks it.

This does not turn the Hidden Way into a useful portal. The trap remains a lie. The victory is not that the road was secretly true. The victory is that the party survives what was built to destroy them.

## Final Movement

The game does not have a fourth act.

After the Hidden Way creature is defeated, the party is ripped back down the spine of the vast creature of crystal and light. This return is violent, involuntary, and mythic. They are dragged through the road's own body, through the places that studied them, and hurled back toward the beginning.

They tumble onto the earth outside the burning walls of Greyharbour.

The emotional movement of the ending is:

1. Disbelief.
2. Failure.
3. Recognition.
4. Resolution.
5. Power.
6. Joy.

For a moment, the party believes they have returned empty-handed.

There is no surface. No dawn. No hidden kingdom. No external salvation. The harbour is burning in front of them. They are ash-covered, bruised, exhausted, and unable to pretend that the old story was true.

Then they look at each other.

They see what they have become: seven figures who crossed the entire false cosmos and returned. They are not carrying salvation as an object. They have become enough to attempt it.

The emotional turn should be slow. It is not a speech first. It is mutual recognition:

- Danica sees Duncan still standing.
- Tara laughs once because she is alive.
- Xavier sees that the beautiful thing was the company, not the road.
- Tahrone understands that the Court can no longer name what they are.
- Kestrel counts the people near them and keeps the dark off the edges.
- The PC sees all six, and all six see the PC.

Then Greyharbour sees them.

This is the crescendo: not cheerful victory, but fierce, astonished joy. The party did not find salvation. They became the last form of it available.

## The Seven and the Seven Ships

The ending image is seven heroes and seven remaining ships.

The party has returned to a harbour that is no longer defensible in the old sense. The final objective is not to restore the city as it was. It is to prevent the last free people from being extinguished.

Each of the seven heroes becomes a living lantern, protector, saint, captain, or terrible sign for one of the remaining ships.

The departure across the Black Sea should not feel like a clean plan solved by a magic item. It should feel like a communal leap into darkness made possible because the seven have returned transformed.

The final story movement:

- The party falls outside Greyharbour.
- They feel the full grief of failure.
- They recognize one another as changed, wise, powerful, and alive.
- They fight the final monster at the gates or harbour approach.
- The seven remaining ships launch.
- The seven heroes lead Greyharbour into the Black Sea.

Useful formulation:

> They went out looking for a light. They came back as seven lights.

Another useful formulation:

> There was no road to the surface. So they took the sea.

## The Final Monster

There should be one final monster at Greyharbour, not a whole return act and not a tour of boss-guarded teleport sites.

This creature should represent the Bone Court's final claim on the harbour. It is not merely a random siege beast. It stands between the transformed party and the evacuation/departure of the last free people.

Possible direction:

- A Bone Court siege-saint made from drowned Netbound, dead dockworkers, confiscated ship-bones, and Court rites.
- A harbour warden that exists to make departure impossible.
- A monster that embodies the Court's argument: freedom ends at the waterline; all routes eventually belong to the dead.

This fight is not about obtaining a reward. It is the moment the party proves that their return matters.

## Greyharbour and the Black Sea

Greyharbour's deepest possibility is not the surface. It is departure.

The city is a harbour, not only a wall. Its oldest practical hope is that ships can still leave.

This should be seeded early:

- The seven remaining ships exist as a known fact, rumour, or forbidden contingency.
- Some harbour officials maintain sealed stores, old launch protocols, hidden crew rolls, or coded lists.
- The idea of taking the Black Sea is considered desperate, shameful, mad, or impossible.
- Nobody believes it can work because there is no reliable light, no known landfall, and no guarantee of return.

By the end, the question is not whether the Black Sea plan is rational. It is whether the last free people will submit, burn, or depart.

The transformed party gives the departure enough symbolic and practical force to happen.

## Act I Party and Major Character Arcs

### Xavier, Assassin Rogue

Archetype: pale bookworm assassin, romantic daydreamer, impossible hands.

Surface read: Xavier is quiet, pasty, slender, and slightly unreal-looking. Androgynous, long-fingered, physically delicate at first glance, with the wrong kind of stillness. He looks like someone who should be shelving books, not putting a blade exactly between someone's ribs.

He is obsessed with romance novels: tragic lovers, secret vows, masked strangers, moonlit balconies that obviously do not exist in a sunless underground world, doomed letters, impossible reunions. He lives partly inside that literature. Not because he is stupid, and not because he is a sap or melancholic, but because books have given him a private emotional vocabulary for something he already believes.

Xavier believes in beauty, truth, love, and kindness. He is as much a believer as Danica and Duncan are. Duncan believes through faith, Danica believes through oath, and Xavier believes through beauty.

Background: Xavier is an orphan, but that is not the wound and should not be treated as the centre of his character. He grew up without a family and has mostly accepted that as a basic fact. It gave him independence, odd habits, and too much time alone with books.

The formative event is that he was rescued from the dark waters as a child.

He remembers the terror of being in the black water, the cold, the soundlessness under the surface, and the feeling of being pulled down. But what he remembers most is being pulled upward into lamplight by a mother and daughter.

He remembers their faces. The mother's face. The daughter's face. Wet hair, hard hands, breathless effort, a lantern shaking somewhere behind them. More than their faces, he remembers their conviction: the absolute certainty that he should be saved, that he was worth hauling out of the dark.

That is why he believes in beauty and kindness. Not because books lied to him, and not because he is naive. Because his first true memory is that the world, for one moment, was exactly as beautiful as a story: hands reaching down, light above, strangers refusing to let him vanish.

The romance novels came later and gave language to something he already knew.

When people call him "boy," he does not care. It is not worth defending his dignity in that register. He has seen something more important than being taken seriously. He has seen kindness act with force.

Combat identity: Assassin Rogue because that keeps him clean and legible. He is not a gadgeteer and not a broad comic thief. He is precise, quiet, fast, and unnervingly dexterous. The joke is not that he is secretly dangerous; the joke is that he is obviously dangerous if anyone paid attention, but people are distracted by how dreamy and harmless he seems.

Voice: Soft, distracted, florid when romance-brained, then suddenly exact.

Example tone:

> "I had always imagined betrayal would involve more candlelight. But this is fine."

#### Anxiety

Xavier has an anxiety around dark water.

Useful triggers:

- Black water.
- Drowning sounds.
- Enclosed boats.
- Sudden cold.
- Being pulled under.
- Silence after splashing.

But he is not defined by fear. He is defined by the rescue.

#### Arc

Xavier's arc is not "daydreamer learns reality is ugly."

It is:

> Believer in beauty learns that beauty is not found; it is enacted.

He has learned emotion through stories. He understands longing, sacrifice, devotion, tragedy, and beauty as literary shapes before he fully understands them as duties to actual people. At the start, he may treat danger almost like a scene he has wandered into. He is brave, but in a strangely aesthetic way.

His flaw is not cowardice. It is unreality.

He wants life to become legible like a book. He wants love and death to have symmetry. He wants people to reveal themselves in perfect speeches. But Greyharbour, the Court, and the Hidden Way are messier than that. People die badly. Betrayal is petty. Love is practical. Courage is often ugly. Nobody has the right line at the right moment.

His movement is from:

> "Life becomes meaningful when it resembles the stories."

to:

> "The stories were rehearsal. The real vow is what you do when nothing is beautiful yet."

The Hidden Way is the ultimate false romance: ancient road, promised light, sacred destination, beautiful lie. Xavier should be especially vulnerable to it. He wants the legend to have shape. He wants the ending to redeem the suffering.

When the surface is false, it breaks something important in him. Not his orphanhood, not his self-esteem. His idea that beauty guarantees meaning.

But his final turn is not cynicism. It is deeper belief.

He realizes that the beautiful thing was never the road. It was the people who walked it without betraying one another. It was Tara's filthy courage, Danica's oath, Duncan's mercy, Kestrel's protective light, Tahrone's terrible honesty, the PC's endurance.

The books were not wrong because the world failed to resemble them. The books were training him to recognize what must be done when the world refuses beauty.

Core line:

> "Then we shall make it true."

#### Party Friction

With Tara:

- Tara thinks he is ridiculous.
- Xavier thinks Tara is vulgar but secretly magnificent.
- Tara calls his books filth or nonsense.
- Xavier correctly identifies that Tara is the most romantic person in the group and she hates him for it.

With Danica:

- He admires Danica's oath as if it were literary: pure force, clean shape, awful devotion.
- Danica may find this shallow until he proves he understands vows in action, not just in beautiful phrasing.

With Tahrone:

- He is less instinctively disgusted by Tahrone than Tara is.
- He may initially romanticize Tahrone's necromancy as gothic tragedy, which is morally dangerous and useful for party contrast.
- Tahrone may make him understand that form, rite, and memory are not aesthetic ornaments; they are obligations.

With Duncan:

- Duncan may be one of the first to see that Xavier's daydreaming is not stupidity. It is longing with an object: the conviction that kindness can be made real here and now.

With Kestrel:

- Xavier is tempted to romanticize Kestrel's suffering because it resembles the beautiful tragic shapes he knows from books.
- His growth requires him to stop seeing their suffering as beautiful and start seeing their freedom as sacred.
- Kestrel may trust him more once he learns to look away at the right times.

#### Endgame Xavier

At Greyharbour, Xavier becomes for others what the mother and daughter were for him: a face above the dark water, certain that they are worth saving.

His ship should be the one full of frightened children, scribes, apprentices, wounded people, and quiet passengers who need someone calm.

Not because he is warm in an obvious way, but because he knows how to turn terror into a story people can survive for one more hour.

Final Xavier energy:

> "No, no. This is not the end. It is plainly the chapter where everyone believes it is the end. Very different thing. Keep walking."

At the rail, when the Black Sea is too much:

> "Look at me. Not the water. Me. You are coming up."

### Brassica, Dirt Wizard

Status: major Act I character, not one of the final seven. Possible future protagonist.

Archetype: self-taught wizard, refinery hermit, first-principles obsessive, filthy autodidact.

The phrase "dirt wizard" is not self-loathing here. It is pride. It means: I did not learn through the necromantic empire's schools, permissions, robes, approved vocabularies, or dead-hand traditions. I learned because the world is readable if you are stubborn enough to read it.

Her core belief:

> Everything hard-won is best-won.

That is almost her religion.

She believes knowledge gained easily is usually borrowed, softened, compromised, or inherited with someone else's assumptions hidden inside it. Knowledge earned the hard way has moral weight. If you have to translate the book yourself, burn your fingers on the experiment, rebuild the principle from first causes, and sleep under the table because you forgot time existed, then maybe you actually know something.

#### Surface Read

Brassica is below average height, black-haired, short-cropped, and permanently in the wrong state of bodily maintenance. Not disgusting as a performance, and not because she hates herself. She simply does not think bathing is more urgent than solving the thing currently eating her mind.

She wears an old librarian's robe she acquired somewhere. Probably not stole, exactly. Acquired. Found. Inherited from a room that no longer had institutional use for it.

The robe is covered in notes. Some are stitched in. Some are inked. Some are burned into the fabric in tiny, precise handwriting. The sleeves are partly mnemonic devices. If she needs to remember a binding sequence, a dead language suffix, or the correction to a bad translation, it may be on her cuff.

When she is cold, she does not choose warmer clothing. She puts on another identical librarian robe. Then another. This is not eccentricity for flavour; it tells us how she thinks. A problem exists. Add another layer of the same solution until the problem stops.

#### Social Bearing

Brassica is a loner by choice, but not because she despises people. She leans out of social life because she does not intuitively understand its rhythms, and because she is always preoccupied by something more definite than conversation.

Xavier leans toward the social world because he believes in beauty between people.

Brassica leans away because the truth of a thing is usually quieter than a room full of people.

She can seem aloof, rude, or cruel, but often because she is describing the world at the wrong level of abstraction. If someone says, "My brother died," Brassica may first think about cause, mechanism, witness reliability, possible contamination, ritual consequence. She is not indifferent. She is arriving at care through the wrong door.

She is deeply stung by dishonesty and crudity.

Not "crudity" as in swearing. Tara can swear all day and Brassica may not care. Crudity means intellectual crudity: lazy categories, false simplifications, pretending to know, using language to obscure rather than reveal.

Dishonesty wounds her because it contaminates the work. A lie is not only morally bad; it damages the possibility of understanding. It puts grit in the instrument.

This gives her a good flaw. She can be harsh because she treats imprecision as betrayal.

#### Background

Brassica comes from a rich-quarter family in Greyharbour. They are among the last remnants of old money, status, and house-pride inside the city.

She is estranged from them and feels no active guilt about it. That matters. Her arc should not be "learn to love her family." Too easy, and wrong for her.

Her family represents inherited position, polish, acceptable education, and the idea that knowledge should confirm rank. Brassica walked away from all of that. Not dramatically, perhaps. She may simply have found it intellectually unserious.

At the end of the game, when her family sees her again, they gasp. Not because she has become socially redeemed or prettied up. Because this small, filthy, impossible person has become a being of terrifying resolution and power. They finally see the thing they failed to recognize: not a failed daughter of the house, but a mind that made itself sovereign.

#### Knowledge Hunger

Brassica will go almost any length to understand something.

That is both noble and dangerous.

She does not want knowledge as status. She wants the truth of the thing itself. If there is a poisoned oil, she wants to know not just who poisoned it but how the poison participates in the oil, what principle it exploits, what old language names it, what happens if it is heated, whether the Court invented it or stole it, whether lampfish bodies resist it, whether the effect can be inverted.

She burrows.

That makes her perfect for Act I's oil/refinery material. She is the person who proves the sabotage is not merely bad supply or dockside corruption. Something has been done to the light.

#### Arc

Brassica's trajectory is Hegelian in the broad sense: from abstract truth to concrete truth.

She begins by trusting what can be isolated, derived, translated, tested, reconstructed. She is not wrong. That mode of thought is powerful, and it is part of why she survives. But it is incomplete because it tries to understand life by stripping away relation, circumstance, and embodiment.

Her movement:

1. Abstract: truth as principle, mechanism, language, proof.
2. Negation: the world violates the clean model through pain, loyalty, torture, courage, grief, beauty, love.
3. Concrete: truth as embodied action; knowledge made real in how one lives, protects, heals, refuses, and acts with others.

Brassica does not abandon intellect. She becomes more fully intellectual because her thought finally has to include the living world.

Her arc is not "loner learns friendship." That is too generic.

Better:

> Brassica begins by believing that truth is best approached alone, because other people introduce error.

She ends by understanding that some truths only appear in relation: oath, grief, beauty, courage, faith, loyalty, sacrifice. Not because those things are sentimental, but because they are real phenomena and she has been excluding them from her model.

Her movement can be summarized as:

> "Everything true can be derived from first principles if I am sufficiently exact."

to:

> "Some first principles are people."

The Hidden Way should hit her differently from Xavier. Xavier is wounded because the beautiful story is false. Brassica is enraged because the entire system is an engineered falsehood masquerading as metaphysics. A lie that has lasted ages. A bad proof with bones under it.

At the end, her joy is not soft. It is the joy of seeing a new theorem become possible:

The Court's world is not closed.

The false road can be survived.

The seven are now evidence.

#### Future-Use: The Kestrel Scene

This material is preserved as future or alternate Brassica material. Brassica is not currently one of the seven, so this is not part of the main party spine unless she is brought back into direct contact with Kestrel later.

If Brassica encounters Kestrel after their torture by the Bone Court, her first instinct is diagnostic: restraints, wounds, ritual cuts, light extraction, nerve damage, magical residue, what was done, how long, for what purpose.

Then something breaks through. Not sentiment. Contact.

Kestrel is not a specimen of Court technique. They are a person in pain, right there, still alive, still looking back.

Brassica is shocked and troubled because her usual mode fails her for a second. She can understand the apparatus, but not the fact of suffering as an immediate demand. The question is no longer "what is this?" but "what must I do?"

That is the beginning of her concrete turn.

Brassica starts listing observations too fast. Someone snaps at her. A healer moves to Kestrel. Someone else, maybe unusually quiet, watches Brassica realize she is not being precise. She is hiding in precision.

Then Brassica acts. Awkwardly, abruptly, without the right words. She cuts a binding, burns a sigil, places her own robe under Kestrel's head, ruins an irreplaceable note-sleeve to stop bleeding or interrupt a rite.

That action matters because she sacrifices abstraction materially. The notes on the robe are knowledge. Burning or tearing them to save someone means the knowledge has become concrete.

The arc in miniature:

> The truth of the spell was written on her sleeve.
> The truth of the moment was that the sleeve had to be torn.

#### Not Softened

Brassica does not become socially fluent, tender, or "finally normal." That would ruin her. She remains alien. The change is not that the gap closes. The change is that she understands the gap as work.

At the beginning, the gap between Brassica and the world feels almost ontological: she is over here with truth, language, proofs, systems; people are over there making noise, lying badly, bleeding unpredictably, and interrupting thought.

By the end, the gap is still there, but it has become practical:

> There is distance between me and the world. Therefore I must build a bridge, a procedure, an act, a spell, a correction, a rescue.

She is not softened by love. She is powered by it.

Love does not make her gentle. It gives her an inexhaustible task orientation. Someone is hurt; therefore the world is wrongly arranged; therefore rearrange it.

A good formulation:

> Brassica does not become warm. She becomes answerable.

Or:

> Love does not humanize Brassica. It gives the machine an object.

She is a fierce and terrible machine powered to act, in her own peculiar way, by love.

#### Endgame Brassica

At the end, her family sees her outside the burning city and cannot reconcile her with the person they dismissed. She is ash-covered, layered in scorched robes, sleeve-notes glowing or burned through, hair hacked short, eyes bright with exhaustion and impossible certainty.

She barely notices their amazement. Or she notices and files it away as socially significant but not presently useful.

They do not see "the strange girl became nice." They see the strange girl became a principle with hands. A living proof that loves them enough to work without stopping.

Her ship should carry makers, refinery workers, clerks, apprentices, engineers, people who can keep systems alive. She is not comforting in Xavier's way. She is terrifyingly competent.

Final Brassica energy:

> "Do not pray at the hull. Check the seams. Then pray, if it improves your hands."

Or:

> "Stand behind me. I have understood the problem."

Or:

> "This can be made survivable."

### Duncan, Lantern Domain Cleric

Archetype: harbour priest, peacemaker, cook, mender, group matriarch.

Duncan is the priest of what remains of the harbour's faith, but his holiness is practical before it is ceremonial. He cooks. He patches clothes. He cleans wounds. He notices who has not eaten. He knows which argument needs theology and which one needs soup.

He is good with a needle, probably better than anyone expects. Sails, sleeves, skin, blankets, torn packs: if something can be mended, Duncan will sit with it.

That gives him a strong Lantern Cleric identity: light as care, continuity, warmth, repair. Not just radiant damage. The God's work is keeping bodies alive long enough to choose courage.

He is the matriarch of the group in the old practical sense: the one who creates a livable world around the mission.

He is older, saintly, and deeply associated with Greyharbour's care, light, and protection.

Duncan initially seems like the person who would stay. His whole life has been devoted to protecting the harbour where it stands.

His turn is crucial: he chooses to go in order to protect the last of Greyharbour. This shocks Danica into a deeper and more serious commitment.

#### Duncan and Danica

His relationship to Danica is central:

- Danica sharpens weapons.
- Duncan sharpens needles.
- Danica sleeps in the cold.
- Duncan leaves the blanket.
- Danica answers fear with violence.
- Duncan answers fear with food, stitching, and steadiness.
- Danica thinks mercy might be delay.
- Duncan shows that mercy is labour.

He should be gentle but not weak. Peacemaker does not mean conflict-avoidant. It means he knows how much work peace costs.

Key line:

> It might be a legend, a fanciful story, but I have lived my life in keeping with such a story, and it has granted safety and succour to all here. May the God strengthen our bodies for the work ahead of us.

Example Duncan lines:

> "Sit down. Eat first. Then tell me which unforgivable thing must be done by morning."

To Danica:

> "I did not raise you to be harmless. I raised you to know what harm is for."

#### Party Friction

With Tara:

- Tara tests Duncan's patience by refusing care until it is disguised as food, mending, or a practical order.
- Duncan sees that Tara's profanity is often a form of devotion, and he does not mistake it for emptiness.
- Tara may trust him because he never asks her to become respectable before feeding her.

With Xavier:

- Duncan may be one of the first to see that Xavier's daydreaming is not stupidity. It is longing with an object: the conviction that kindness can be made real here and now.
- Xavier's belief in beauty may move Duncan because it resembles faith without church language.

With Danica:

- Danica sharpens weapons.
- Duncan sharpens needles.
- Danica sleeps in the cold.
- Duncan leaves the blanket.
- Danica answers fear with violence.
- Duncan answers fear with food, stitching, and steadiness.
- Danica thinks mercy might be delay.
- Duncan shows that mercy is labour.

With Tahrone:

- Mutual respect, but tension.
- Duncan treats death through mercy, grief, and continuity.
- Tahrone treats death through form, obligation, and completion.
- They may understand each other better than expected.

With Kestrel:

- Duncan's care is probably the first care that does not aestheticize Kestrel.
- He feeds them, sews for them, lets them sit unseen.
- This may be unbearable at first.

Arc:

- Begins as the harbour's saintly protector.
- Chooses the road not because he abandons Greyharbour, but because staying is no longer enough to protect it.
- At the end, his faith is not validated as factual information. The Hidden Way was false. But the courage, discipline, care, and communal strength produced by that faith are real.

### Tara, Duelist Fighter

Archetype: dockside swashbuckler, thief, drunk, free-port knife smile.

Surface read: Tara looks like she has never taken anything seriously in her life. Hair tied back in one rough knot, loose shirt, trousers, light boots, very light armour, no shield. She fights like a duelist who learned half her moves in tavern brawls and the other half running from people she had just robbed.

She drinks too much, laughs too loudly, swears constantly, and treats danger like a chance to show off.

Actual core: Tara is fiercely devoted to Greyharbour. Not abstractly. She loves the docks, the ugly freedom of the place, the right to leave, return, bargain, fight, swear, drink, and belong to nobody's court. Her unseriousness is not emptiness; it is her way of refusing the Court's dead etiquette.

Act I entrance: She is caught in a fight on the docks with conspiratorial types, probably after stealing, overhearing, or exposing something she was not supposed to notice. She insists it was "none of your business" while obviously bleeding for the harbour's business.

Combat identity: Fast, mobile, dirty duelist. No shield. Ripostes, lunges, disarms, taunts, movement tricks, opportunistic strikes. She should feel like a blade in a crowd, not a soldier.

Voice: Profane, funny, deflective. She says emotionally true things by accident, then immediately insults someone to cover it.

Example tone:

> "I'm not dying in a Court ditch because some bone-faced bastard with a dinner bell told me it was tradition."

#### Wound

Tara has a deep repulsion toward necromancy because of what happened in her family.

Her father died. Her mother, broken by grief, tried to raise him. It went wrong. Not necessarily in a melodramatic horror way, though it can have horror in it. The important point is that Tara saw love become refusal. She saw grief become violation. Since then, necromancy does not read to her as "a school of magic." It reads as someone refusing to let the dead be dead.

So when Tahrone joins the party in Act II, Tara does not just distrust her. She is disgusted by her.

#### Arc

Tara's arc is not "necromancy is fine now."

It is more mature than that.

She comes to understand that death is not cleanly separate from life. The dead remain entangled with the living through love, debt, memory, regret, inheritance, rage, and duty. Remembering the dead is not simple. Honouring them is not simple. Letting them go is not simple either.

Her movement is from:

> "Necromancy is always theft from the dead."

to:

> "The dead are already with us. The question is whether we love them, use them, deny them, or listen to what grief is actually asking of us."

That lets her friction with Tahrone become one of the party's best relationships. They do not need to become cosy. They can remain sharp. But Tahrone forces Tara to think, and Tara forces Tahrone to account morally for what she does.

#### Party Friction

With Xavier:

- Tara thinks he is ridiculous.
- Xavier thinks Tara is vulgar but secretly magnificent.
- Tara calls his books filth or nonsense.
- Xavier correctly identifies that Tara is the most romantic person in the group and she hates him for it.

With Duncan:

- Tara tests Duncan's patience by refusing care until it is disguised as food, mending, or a practical order.
- Duncan sees that Tara's profanity is often a form of devotion, and he does not mistake it for emptiness.
- Tara may trust him because he never asks her to become respectable before feeding her.

With Danica:

- Tara tries to provoke Danica because Danica is funny when flustered and terrifying when annoyed.
- Danica disapproves of Tara's drinking, theft, and general relationship to rules.
- Under the noise, they share a simple loyalty: if Greyharbour is threatened, both move first and justify it later.

With Tahrone:

- Tara hates what Tahrone represents.
- Tahrone understands Tara's disgust and does not try to charm her out of it.
- Their arc should become one of the strongest: Tara learns death is entangled with life; Tahrone learns that procedure without love becomes permission for horror.

With Kestrel:

- Tara may treat Kestrel most normally by swearing around them, offering a drink, and refusing to stare.
- This may make Tara unexpectedly safe.
- Tara's bluntness can protect Kestrel from being turned into a precious symbol again.

#### Endgame Tara

At the end, outside burning Greyharbour, Tara is one of the first to laugh. Not because anything is funny. Because she is alive, impossible, ash-covered, and standing with six people who should not exist.

Her ship should probably be the roughest one: dockworkers, thieves, tavern people, orphans, smugglers, people who would never follow a saint but would absolutely follow Tara if she spat blood, raised a blade, and said:

> "Right. Into the dark, then. Try to keep up."

### Danica, Oath of Vengeance Paladin

Archetype: enormous blonde vengeance knight, daughter of the harbour priest, weaponized grief.

Danica is six feet tall, broad-shouldered, already formidable when the story begins. Blonde hair, either braided with severe practicality or left barely managed because hair is not a battlefield priority. She wears a scowl so often that people mistake it for her resting face, which it mostly is.

Her life is defined intellectually by vengeance and justice, but emotionally by family: especially her father, Duncan, the priest of what remains of the harbour's faith.

Her mother died when Danica was young. Danica has never exactly processed that grief. She has transferred it into action: training, striking, guarding, punishing, standing between Greyharbour and anything that comes for it.

For Danica, there is no meaningful gap between conviction and action.

If something is wrong, you move.

If someone threatens the harbour, you break them.

If evil presents itself, reflection is cowardice wearing a scholar's coat.

That makes her frightening, and useful, and emotionally young in ways she would hate to hear described.

#### Daily Texture

Danica gets up first.

Every morning.

She sharpens her weapons.

Not symbolically. Actually. Methodically. Obsessively. The camp wakes to the scrape of blade on stone. This is funny, but it is also exactly her. She begins each day by making herself ready to answer the world.

The bespoke silence spell from Duncan is perfect. Not grand magic. Parental practical magic.

Duncan, exhausted by years of dawn sharpening, has given her some small clerical hush: a little prayer, token, or blessing that wraps the sound. It is loving and funny and tells us a lot about them.

Possible name:

**Father's Hush**

A minor blessing Duncan taught Danica so her morning weapon-sharpening would not wake the whole harbour.

#### Food and Camp

Danica hates trying new food.

Not because she has refined taste. Because new food is unnecessary uncertainty. She has things she eats. They work. Why is everyone making this strange? If Xavier describes a meal as "sensuous," she may leave.

She sleeps away from the fire.

Always on the cold edge of camp, facing outward. Partly to protect the group. Partly because she refuses comfort on principle. Partly because she is emotionally incapable of admitting that being near people feels good.

Her stated reason:

> "I don't need any freaking fire."

Which everyone understands means:

> "If something comes in the night, it reaches me first."

This is great because it makes her love visible through refusal. Danica's tenderness almost always arrives disguised as discipline, irritation, or tactical positioning.

Camp texture:

- Tara offers her a drink; Danica says no like she has been offered poison.
- Xavier offers her some elaborate preserved sweet from a romance-novel region; Danica stares at it like it might be a trap.
- Duncan quietly leaves a blanket near her. She pretends not to notice. It is over her shoulders by morning.
- She sleeps in armour too often.
- She sharpens weapons at dawn under Father's Hush.
- If anyone asks why she sleeps so far away, she says, "Perimeter." Then, after a pause: "And the fire smells weird."

#### Social and Romantic Ineptitude

Danica is catastrophically bad at romance and delicate personal feeling.

Not because she is cold. Because she is too direct for the indirect rituals of affection. If she likes someone, she becomes tactical, overprotective, or abruptly silent. If someone flirts with her, she may look like she is preparing to counterattack.

She can face a Court executioner without blinking, but a compliment makes her furious with embarrassment.

This keeps her from becoming one-note grim. She is terrifying in combat, absolute in conviction, and then completely defeated by someone saying, "Your hair looks nice."

#### Arc

Danica's starting point:

> "Love means destroying what threatens the beloved."

Her ending point:

> "Love means carrying the beloved forward, even when vengeance would be simpler."

That is the whole thing.

She does not stop being violent. She does not become moderate in some boring way. She remains a weapon of awful force. But the purpose of the weapon changes.

At the start, she wants to leave and take the fight to the Court because staying feels like passivity. Duncan represents care, mercy, endurance, the harbour, the people. Danica loves him, but she may partly see his way as too slow.

Then Duncan chooses to go.

That shocks her because it means the harbour's mercy is not passive. Her father's faith is not refusal to act. It is the deeper courage to act for preservation rather than hatred.

Danica's vengeance grows up into guardianship.

Not softer. Larger.

Her arc is not "learn comfort." It is better if she remains ridiculous about comfort. But by the end, maybe she lets others stand near her. That is enough.

#### Party Friction

With Tara:

- Tara tries to provoke Danica because Danica is funny when flustered and terrifying when annoyed.
- Danica disapproves of Tara's drinking, theft, and general relationship to rules.
- Under the noise, they share a simple loyalty: if Greyharbour is threatened, both move first and justify it later.

With Xavier:

- Xavier admires Danica's oath as if it were literary: pure force, clean shape, awful devotion.
- Danica may find this shallow until he proves he understands vows in action, not just in beautiful phrasing.
- Xavier's romantic attention embarrasses her more efficiently than danger ever could.

With Duncan:

- Danica sharpens weapons.
- Duncan sharpens needles.
- Danica sleeps in the cold.
- Duncan leaves the blanket.
- Danica answers fear with violence.
- Duncan answers fear with food, stitching, and steadiness.
- Danica thinks mercy might be delay.
- Duncan shows that mercy is labour.

With Tahrone:

- Danica may want to kill her on principle.
- Tahrone does not plead.
- She simply states what she has done and accepts the moral danger of being judged.
- Danica must learn that judgement is not the same as immediate execution.

With Kestrel:

- Danica wants to kill whoever did this.
- Kestrel might find that simple and comforting.
- Or frightening.
- Danica has to learn that protecting Kestrel includes letting them decide what protection means.

Endgame Danica should be almost mythic: a huge ash-covered paladin standing at the prow or gangplank, not as the daughter who wants revenge, but as the shield that moves.

Final Danica energy:

> "Behind me. All of you."

Or, if someone asks whether she is afraid:

> "Yes. Move."

## Act II Companion Arcs

### Tahrone, Necromancer Wizard

Former office: High Chair of the Pale.

Current recognition: still a Chair of the Pale, but no longer the High Chair. She corrects people who bow too deeply or use the old title.

That correction should be very telling:

> "Chair Tahrone is sufficient. The higher title has been rescinded."

Not bitter. Exact.

#### Core

Tahrone believes death is woven through life with fine filigree thread. Not crudely, not as domination, not as corpse-power for its own sake. Death is continuity, inheritance, obligation, record, debt, law, transition, and form.

Her doctrine:

> No one dies unnamed, unknown, and unremembered.

And:

> To die is not to become a thing. It is to become a sacred memory.

Her original loyalty to the Bone Court came from this. She believed the Court mastered chaos. It gave death a place. It prevented waste, panic, hunger, and disorder. It made the terrible legible.

So her old belief was:

> The Court is correct because the Court preserves order.

What breaks her is discovering that the Court's order is not order at all. It is appetite wearing law.

#### The Kestrel Incident

A rival Court, more liberal, more unhinged, more hostile to light, captures Kestrel and tortures them.

This is not merely "cruel," though it is cruel. To Tahrone it is a violation of form.

They have taken a living source of light and treated them as material. They are experimenting without proper end, without reverence, without legal boundary, without completion. They are leaving rituals unfinished, bodies suspended between categories, pain prolonged beyond purpose.

For Tahrone, this is obscene.

If the Court turns persons into things, then it is not the guardian of death. It is death's desecrator.

So she hides Kestrel.

Then, for a short time, she attempts the impossible: continuing as High Chair while concealing the evidence that has broken her faith. A week, maybe slightly more. Long enough to show that her first instinct is still procedural containment: document, conceal, stabilize, assess, prepare accusation.

Then she denounces the other Court.

The denunciation destroys her office. She is removed as High Chair, held under suspicion, watched, and politically isolated. No one can prove she hid Kestrel, but everyone who matters suspects it.

That is exactly when the party meets her: not as a comfortable insider, and not yet as an open rebel, but as someone whose whole metaphysical order has cracked.

#### Behaviour

Tahrone is immaculately polite and controlled.

Not warm. Not casual. Not theatrical. She speaks like someone who believes the right word prevents moral collapse.

She has a proper way for everything:

- How to address the dead.
- How to close a ritual.
- How to accuse someone.
- How to receive a guest.
- How to dispose of a body.
- How to lay out surgical instruments.
- How to apologize.
- How to defect.

No ritual should ever be left unfinished.

That phrase should matter mechanically and narratively. It is how she thinks about magic, law, relationships, betrayal, and eventually the party.

#### Curiosity

She does not dismiss alternative practices. She is curious about them, but only when they have process and purpose.

She might find Duncan's faith fascinating because it is disciplined care.

She might find Brassica unbearable and compelling because Brassica is all first-principles process with no social form.

She might find Xavier's romance novels absurd until she realizes they are also ritual structures: vow, obstacle, sacrifice, recognition, union.

She will clash with Tara because Tara will see her as Court rot in a silk glove.

#### Arc

Tahrone's arc is not "learn death is bad."

Her movement is:

> Order as hierarchy, office, law, ritual correctness.

to:

> Order as responsibility, completion, witness, and protection.

She does not abandon necromancy. She purifies her understanding of it by severing it from Court legitimacy.

Her core realization:

> A system that permits desecration for power has no right to call itself order.

By the end, she should still be controlled, formal, and terrifying. But now her formality is in service of the living company, not the Bone Court.

#### Party Friction

With Tara:

- Tara hates what Tahrone represents.
- Tahrone understands Tara's disgust and does not try to charm her out of it.
- Their arc should become one of the strongest: Tara learns death is entangled with life; Tahrone learns that procedure without love becomes permission for horror.

Tahrone's answer to Tara is not "you are wrong," but:

> "I have seen what happens when the dead are left to power. They are counted, rendered, assigned, spent. Memory is not sentiment. It is defence."

With Duncan:

- Mutual respect, but tension.
- Duncan treats death through mercy, grief, and continuity.
- Tahrone treats death through form, obligation, and completion.
- They may understand each other better than expected.

With Xavier:

- Xavier is less instinctively disgusted by Tahrone than Tara is.
- He may initially romanticize Tahrone's necromancy as gothic tragedy, which is morally dangerous and useful for party contrast.
- Tahrone may make him understand that form, rite, and memory are not aesthetic ornaments; they are obligations.
- Xavier may make Tahrone confront the possibility that beauty is not disorder.

With Danica:

- Danica may want to kill her on principle.
- Tahrone does not plead.
- She simply states what she has done and accepts the moral danger of being judged.

With Kestrel:

- This is the central bond.
- Tahrone saved them, but also remained inside the system for as long as she could.
- Kestrel may owe her life and still not forgive her.
- Tahrone may not ask forgiveness because asking would be another demand placed on someone already tortured.

That last point is very important. Tahrone's restraint should be part of her moral seriousness.

Possible line to Kestrel:

> "You owe me nothing. Not gratitude. Not absolution. Not even accuracy, if accuracy is more than you can bear."

#### Endgame Tahrone

At the end, Tahrone is not redeemed into softness. She becomes a black-robed legal-mystical terror on behalf of the departing people.

Her ship should carry the dead, the dying, the old records, names, ashes, legal seals, funerary bundles, and the people most afraid they will be forgotten.

Her final role: no one leaves unnamed. No death is wasted. No life is processed into Court property.

Final Tahrone energy:

> "By witness, by name, by breath remaining: these people are not yours."

Or:

> "This rite is not concluded. Stand aside."


### Kestrel, Lantern Patron Warlock

Pronouns: they/them.

Type: former Court pet, now gentle monster.

Kestrel is non-binary and should be written as non-binary as possible: not vague, not coy, not a puzzle to be solved. They are simply Kestrel.

Core wound:

> Belonging can be administratively revoked.

#### Class Group

Kestrel was raised inside the Court's communal child system, not a traditional family. The Court does not uphold family in the ordinary sense. Children are raised communally, sorted, educated, observed, and attached to class groups, cohorts, or houses.

Kestrel's "family" was a class group: peers, tutors, attendants, and older wardens, the small social body that knew them intimately.

They were the people who knew how Kestrel slept, when they were frightened, what made them laugh, how to hide their brightness, how to make them seem ordinary during inspections.

They protected Kestrel through small collective acts:

- Dimming lamps before inspectors arrived.
- Misreporting symptoms.
- Swapping records.
- Distracting tutors.
- Teaching them to breathe their light down.
- Hiding burned sheets or cracked glass.
- Giving them ordinary tasks so they looked less exceptional.
- Lying badly but loyally.

Then the institution reclassified Kestrel out of that belonging.

Not kidnapped in a dramatic alley. Advanced. Transferred. Honoured.

The Court language:

> "Their needs exceed cohort provision."

> "The School can provide proper care."

> "Do not confuse attachment with suitability."

That is the wound. Not "nobody loved me." Worse:

> The people who loved me were never allowed to be enough.

#### Court Pet and School

Kestrel was taken into the care of a more liberal, aesthetically adventurous Court. Publicly, they were displayed as a living lantern: dressed beautifully, praised, protected, admired, called rare and precious.

They were not treated like a crude prisoner at first. They were admired into captivity.

The Court's language around them was probably full of affection:

- "Our bright one."
- "The little lantern."
- "A miracle under proper care."
- "Too delicate for the vulgar world."
- "A guest of honour."

But the truth was ownership.

When Kestrel's Lantern patron, power, or inner self would not submit to Court theology, they were transferred to the School.

The School is where affection becomes experiment.

White tiles, careful notes, senior researchers, beautiful language, exact cruelty. It calls pain "response," captivity "care," and violation "clarification."

The Court wanted to know whether living light could be made obedient.

#### Current Shape

Kestrel is gentle by temperament, monstrous by consequence.

The torture changed their pact or body. Their light does not merely illuminate. It burns, reveals, scars, unsettles dead matter, makes lies visible, causes old wounds to glow, perhaps makes people remember what they buried.

They do not want to hurt anyone.

Their presence can hurt.

They do not want to be worshipped.

Their light makes people kneel.

They do not want to be looked at.

Darkness recoils from them.

They hate being called hope.

They are, unfortunately, necessary.

#### Tahrone

Tahrone discovers what has been done, hides Kestrel, denounces the other Court, and loses the High Chair.

Tahrone saved Kestrel using the same administrative world that stole them. She knows the forms, seals, custodial phrases, and transfer protocols. She can hide Kestrel because she understands exactly how persons are turned into cases.

Kestrel may hate that they needed someone like Tahrone to survive.

Their bond is debt without forgiveness, intimacy without ease.

Kestrel may trust Tahrone least and rely on her most.

#### Arc

Kestrel's final state is not messianic. They do not become a radiant answer everyone can stare at. That would repeat the Court's mistake: turning them into a symbol.

Their final state is belonging.

They begin as someone whose light made them exceptional, separated, displayed, extracted. The Court isolated them by making them precious. The School tortured them by making them useful. Even rescue risks making them special again.

The party's gift is that Kestrel becomes part of a group where their light matters, but does not define their whole personhood.

That echoes childhood correctly. Their first belonging was the class group: imperfect, practical, protective, mutual. They lose that when the Court administratively removes them. In the party, they find a second cohort. Not a replacement family in a sentimental way, but a living group that keeps choosing them after law, fear, and usefulness say otherwise.

Their belonging arc:

> "All belonging can be administratively revoked."

to:

> "Belonging is what people continue to do after law says it is over."

Their light arc:

> "My light is what they used to own me."

to:

> "My light is how I keep my people from being left in the dark."

That is not messianic. It is intimate, practical, and protective.

#### Party Friction

With Tahrone:

- Tahrone hid them.
- Tahrone may be the only Court figure who treated their personhood as procedurally real.
- Kestrel may trust her least and rely on her most.
- Their bond is debt without forgiveness, intimacy without ease.

With Duncan:

- Duncan's care is probably the first care that does not aestheticize them.
- He feeds them, sews for them, lets them sit unseen.
- This may be unbearable at first.

With Danica:

- Danica wants to kill whoever did this.
- Kestrel might find that simple and comforting.
- Or frightening.

With Xavier:

- He is tempted to romanticize them.
- His growth requires him to stop seeing their suffering as beautiful and start seeing their freedom as sacred.

With Tara:

- Tara may treat them most normally by swearing around them, offering a drink, and refusing to stare.
- This may make Tara unexpectedly safe.

#### Endgame Kestrel

Kestrel's final ship should not be full of worshippers. It should be a cohort: children, stragglers, injured people, frightened apprentices, maybe people who would otherwise be administratively discarded.

Kestrel does not stand above them shining. They move among them, counting heads, touching shoulders only when welcome, keeping the dark off the edges.

The Court made them a living lantern to be looked at.

The party lets them become someone who holds the light for others without disappearing into it.

Final Kestrel energy:

> "You're safe with me."

And:

> "Nobody gets left in the dark while I'm around."

## The Bone Court's Pressure on Greyharbour

The Bone Court wants more than oil, though oil matters.

It wants:

- Control of Greyharbour's lampfish oil and light production.
- Control of the docks, channels, boats, and sea access.
- Control of food and supply routes.
- Political legitimacy: the symbolic submission of the Last Free Port.
- The end of Greyharbour as an example that free life is possible outside Court dependency.
- Access to bodies, dead, funerary customs, and labour.
- The transformation of aid into obligation.

The Court should often appear reasonable before it appears monstrous. Its argument is that Greyharbour cannot survive alone. Its horror is that this may sound true.

## The Hidden Way

The Hidden Way is not a portal plot.

It is a mythic pressure valve and killing machine.

It exists so that powerful beings who might threaten Court rule can be drawn away from political struggle and into a sacred-looking trap. The lie turns rebellion into pilgrimage.

Important rules:

- The Bone Court does not need to fear a chart to the Hidden Way.
- The Court broadly trusts the trap to destroy those who follow it.
- The Hidden Way should feel beautiful, ancient, and meaningful even though it is false.
- The falsehood should hurt because the journey mattered.
- The end must not reveal that the surface was secretly real.

## Rest-Stones

Working design term: rest-stones.

Possible in-world terms to consider:

- Ember Shards.
- Lampstones.
- Saintstones.
- Lumen Stones.

Function during play:

- Long rest.
- Level up.
- Crafting.
- Recovery.
- Overland pacing and survival structure.

Truth:

- The rest-stones are part of the luminous creature/trap at the end of the Hidden Way.
- They have been studying and shaping the party as the party uses them.
- The party's growth happens inside the enemy's system, which makes the final confrontation intimate and strange.

## Design Guardrails

- No Act IV. The finale is a short, forceful final movement after Act III.
- No clean portal choice ending.
- No true surface reveal.
- No external object that simply solves Greyharbour.
- No boss tour across every rest-stone.
- The final choice, if any, is about posture, order, companionship, or who leads which ship, not whether the metaphysics of the world can be fixed.
- The end should be moving because it denies the promised salvation and still finds a form of joy.
- The game is not cynical. Danger, violence, grief, and failure are real, but the story is unapologetically about finding salvation here and now through courage, kindness, loyalty, beauty, and action.

## Current Best Ending Summary

The heroes leave Greyharbour to find a light they can bring back.

They cross the Court, the old lands, and the Hidden Way.

They learn the way is false and that there is no surface.

They defeat the vast creature of crystal and light that has been learning them through the rest-stones.

They are ripped back down its spine and hurled outside the burning walls of Greyharbour.

For a moment, they believe they have failed.

Then they look at one another and understand what they have become.

They fight the final monster at the gates.

Seven heroes lead seven ships into the Black Sea.

They did not find salvation.

They became enough to attempt it.
